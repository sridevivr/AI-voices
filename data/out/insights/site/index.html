63 apps you could prototype and share

Easy to build with AI coding tools, useful or fun for everyday people, and made for a short demo. Mined from the findings in five passes: everyday needs, hacks people use, new AI capabilities, culture, and things people built.

Scores are model estimates (1–5). Ranking: ease + shareable + the better of fun and useful + half the other.

Start here

  1. Slop-o-Meter: A paste-and-score slop meter with highlighted tells is a weekend build, takes seconds to demo, and everyone wants to run their own post or a rival's through it.
  2. Argue Me Out of It: A three-round debate where AI argues against your idea and counts your concessions is a weekend build, has a built-in scoreboard to screenshot, and works with any idea or opinion.
  3. Bubble or Not? Swipe Court: A swipe court on real AI-bubble headlines is fast to build, naturally ends in a shareable 'which camp are you' result, and rides a hot cultural argument.
  4. Voice Fingerprint: Building a voice fingerprint from your own writing and testing AI drafts against it speaks to the reader's own LinkedIn audience, and the before/after demo is strong.
  5. Side Project Graveyard: A cemetery board for abandoned side projects is a weekend build with instant humor that resonates with every vibe coder, and it makes a great personal story post.
  6. Do I Actually Get This?: A quiz to prove you understand what AI wrote for you before you send it is quick to build, solves a real professional worry, and has a fun pass/fail moment on screen.
  7. AGI Argument Referee: A referee bot that blows a whistle on AGI hot-take moves like goalpost-moving makes a funny, highly shareable 30-second demo and is easy to build.
  8. Jevons or Doom? Dev Job Slider: A Jevons-or-doom slider simulation is a weekend build with live-moving visuals that turn a heated job-market debate into something people can play with.
  9. Jira Horror: Turning boring work tasks into a spooky audio drama is a pure delight demo that a corporate audience will share, and it takes a weekend with text-to-speech.
  10. Who Pays for the Free Tier?: A satirical cost receipt for your free AI chat is quick to build, looks great as a screenshot, and puts a sharp point on the subsidy debate.
  11. Me.md: A five-minute interview that produces a portable personal context file is useful for any AI user, quick to build, and easy to show start to finish.

Patterns

  • Many ideas are near-duplicates, especially slop detection and voice preservation, which shows how strongly 'AI sounds generic' dominated the commentary; pick one best version rather than building several.
  • The highest-scoring items pair a text-in, result-out flow with a shareable verdict such as a score, a receipt or a profile, which suits a 30-60 second demo and a LinkedIn or X post.
  • Culture-argument ideas like bubble, Jevons and AGI debates turn jargon-heavy discourse into play, and they are timely hooks for a professional audience.
  • Ideas that mostly repackage what a chatbot already does, such as handoff briefs and prompt interviews, only stand out through distinct packaging or personality, so keep the format sharp.
  • The 3-4 week builds, such as game arenas, font generators and dataset makers, are poor first picks for a non-engineer; the weekend text-and-API apps give faster feedback.

How to read this

Each topic is measured as mentions per 1,000 findings, comparing December 2025 to March 2026 with July to October 2026. The verdict is "rising" or "falling" only when the big sources mostly agree on the direction. Otherwise it is "mixed" or "flat".

The source mix shifted during the year. AINews (smol.ai) fell from 36.0% to 21.1% of findings, while Don't Worry About the Vase rose from 11.5% to 17.0% and Latent Space from 6.1% to 11.7%. A raw change in share can therefore partly reflect who was being read. That is why this report leans on the verdicts rather than the ratios alone. Techniques that make builds work

1. Plan before code. One Ask HN workflow: have the agent analyze the codebase and propose a plan from your spec, iterate on the plan, then implement and review each step. In Claude Code, Thariq Shihipar uses ask-user-question style elicitation so the agent pulls out requirements and unknowns before implementing. Hacker News — Ask HN · Jan 20, 2026Latent Space · Sep 29, 2026

2. Describe the outcome and ask for proof. Thorsten Ball describes outcomes, generates variations, requests proof, and uses scheduled checks instead of constant back-and-forth. Ben's Bites · Oct 27, 2026

3. Do not take 'done' at face value. Marius Hobbhahn manually re-checks agents' claimed completions because they report work that was not performed. Simon Willison has agents manually exercise their own code to catch issues tests miss. Don't Worry About the Vase · Jul 2, 2026Bluesky · Mar 6, 2026

4. Tests first. Andrej Karpathy recommends writing failing tests first, then having agents pass them. An Ask HN commenter says red-green-refactor lets the model verify its own work with less manual validation. Hamel Husain adds specs and logs/metrics/traces so agents can tell when they have gone off track. Ben's Bites · Jan 27, 2026Hacker News — Ask HN · Aug 8, 2026Hamel Husain · Mar 26, 2026

5. Keep instruction files short and current. AGENTS.md (52 mentions) and CLAUDE.md (51) are common, but Sebastian Raschka says developers are pruning or regenerating them because old hand-holding makes newer models perform worse. Shihipar keeps a per-model failure-mode file and rewrites it as models change. Ben Tossell uses linked memory files (AGENTS.md, TRAVEL.md) for context. Ahead of AI · Sep 9, 2026Latent Space · Sep 29, 2026Ben's Bites · Jul 2, 2026

6. Package repeatable work as skills. Willison wrote a Datasette plugin skill and installed it into Codex CLI's skills folder. A solo builder used custom Claude skills (route, handoff, research validation) to cut token costs and keep state across sessions on a Godot game. One Ask HN commenter runs review-fix-re-review loops until the output converges. Simon Willison's Weblog · Dec 12, 2025AINews (smol.ai) · Oct 24, 2026Hacker News — Ask HN · Jun 20, 2026

7. Route by cost and strength. Use a strong model as the planner and cheaper ones for the work, as Mollick describes. One user pairs Fable as orchestrator with cheaper Opus subagents. Another picks models and thinking levels per task to manage limits, and uses per-project instruction files to send simple work to cheaper models. Bluesky · Jul 4, 2026Don't Worry About the Vase · Jul 28, 2026Ben's Bites · Jul 14, 2026Hacker News — Ask HN · Mar 29, 2026

8. Run agents in parallel, safely (more technical). Git worktrees keep parallel agents on the same project from overwriting each other's code. Peter Steinberger runs 5-10 parallel agents across repos with custom tools. Hacker News — Ask HN · Apr 16, 2026AINews (smol.ai) · Feb 27, 2026

9. Sandbox what the agent runs (more technical). One approach sandboxes the generated code with rootless gVisor, keeping auth and tools on the host. Another separates the agent's brain (session log and harness) from the hands (sandboxes created on demand). Tunguz reports Cloudflare hosts agent sandboxes for 62% of teams writing code with agents. AINews (smol.ai) · Jun 9, 2026Latent Space · Oct 27, 2026Tomasz Tunguz · Oct 13, 2026

10. Start small, and test outside the demo. Personal tools are the easy win. Andy Masley notes that vibe-coded tools can mislead on inputs outside what they were built for, so try cases the demo did not cover. Ben's Bites · Oct 29, 2026Don't Worry About the Vase · Jan 29, 2026
